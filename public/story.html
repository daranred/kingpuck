History

A Town, a Goat and Three Days in August

Puck Fair takes place every year from 10 to 12 August in Killorglin, County Kerry, on the banks of the River Laune. Its organisers call it Ireland's oldest festival, with roots in the Gaelic calendar. In Irish it is Aonach an Phoic: the fair of the he-goat.

At its centre is a ritual found nowhere else. A wild mountain goat, a poc, is brought down into town, crowned King by a local girl chosen as Queen Puck, and raised onto a high stand above the town for the three days.

Around him, the whole town becomes the event: horse and cattle fairs, traditional and contemporary music, street performers, markets, a travelling funfair, children's events, packed pubs and twelve hours of free entertainment a day.

It is less a story about a goat than about a town. An old August market became a livestock fair, became a three-day celebration, and somewhere along the way a goat became King.

What happens on each of the three days is on The Fair page.

Four Centuries, Labelled Honestly

There is no single verified origin story. The fair is documented from 1613, evidence suggests an August fair before that, and the most famous story is a legend. Every claim below carries one of four labels.

Documented

Supported by records or reliable sources.

Tradition

Passed down through generations.

Legend

Associated with Puck, not established as fact.

Unknown

The evidence doesn't allow a confident answer.

  1. Before 1613Unknown

    Older Than the Records?

    The earliest layer may be Lughnasa, the Celtic harvest festival held around 1 August, when a male goat could have stood for fertility and a good harvest. As Christianity spread, those customs became tied to Lammas, another harvest feast. It's a strong theory, and an August fair in Killorglin probably predates 1613, but no record links them directly.

  2. 1613Documented

    The Royal Patent

    Jenkyn Conway, the local landowner, receives a royal patent under King James I to hold a fair on Lammas Day and the two days following. It is the earliest solid documentary foundation for Puck Fair, and the cattle fair on 11 August still runs under that charter today.

  3. 1640s–50sLegend

    The Goat Who Warned the Town

    The best-known origin story: a he-goat fled Cromwell's soldiers and raised the alarm in Killorglin. The fair is documented before Cromwell's campaign, so the legend can't explain how it began. Read the legend.

  4. Late 1790sUnknown

    When Did the Crowning Begin?

    Research into the fair's chronology suggests the crowning of a male goat probably began some time after 1795–96, rather than running unbroken from the Cromwellian era.

  5. 1808Tradition

    Tolls and a Loophole

    New law threatened the landlord Harman Blennerhassett's right to collect tolls on cattle, horses and sheep at the August fair. His lawyer, a young Daniel O'Connell, is said to have noticed it didn't mention goats, so the fair became a goat fair, and on 10 August 1808 a goat was hoisted onto a platform to make the point. We are looking for primary records.

  6. 1840sDocumented

    A Goat Is Crowned

    Reports of a goat actually being crowned King become consistent from the 1840s onward.

  7. 1900sDocumented

    A Festival Known Far Beyond Kerry

    Puck draws visitors, musicians, photographers and writers from across Ireland and abroad. The crowds give rise to the town's famous tradition of pubs and brewery tents open around the clock for the three days.

  8. 2013Documented

    Four Hundred Years

    Killorglin marks 400 years since the 1613 patent.

  9. TodayDocumented

    The Tradition Continues

    A goat handler catches a wild goat in the mountains, a new Queen crowns him every 10 August, and on the 12th he is dethroned and returned home. The organisers say the goat's welfare comes first, with an independent veterinarian overseeing it.

Three layers, not one story. An ancient Celtic layer (Lughnasa, the harvest, the symbolic goat). A historical layer (a documented August fair, chartered in 1613). And the King Puck legend, with the later tradition of crowning a real goat. The story told today isn't necessarily the origin of Puck Fair. It's a mythology that grew around a much older fair.

The Honest Answer: Nobody Knows for Sure

Puck is the male goat, and Puck Fair is simply the fair of the he-goat. Why he became King has three answers.

The oldest: at a harvest-time gathering, a male goat stood for fertility and plenty. Theory

The most famous: he warned the town of Cromwell's army, and the crown is the town's thanks. Legend

The most practical: in 1808 the fair became a goat fair to escape a toll on other livestock, and a goat was raised on a platform to prove it. Tradition

What is certain is that the goat is wild, from the mountains around Killorglin, and that the town has crowned one every August for well over a century and a half.

The Mountain, the Goat, the Warning Legend

The story is told like this. During Oliver Cromwell's campaign in Ireland, his soldiers were moving through the country below the McGillycuddy's Reeks when they came upon a herd of wild goats and scattered them.

One male goat, the Puck, broke away. Instead of running up into the mountains with the others, he ran towards Killorglin, exhausted and frightened. When the townspeople saw him arrive they knew danger was coming. They protected their livestock, prepared themselves, and escaped the worst of it.

They never forgot what the goat had done. They began to honour him every year, and in time he became King Puck.

It's a fantastic story, but there is no contemporary evidence for it, and the fair is documented before Cromwell's campaign, so it can't explain how the fair began. It is the most-told story about Puck, and stories are part of the archive too. More legends and lore.
