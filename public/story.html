A Town, a Goat and Three Days in August

Puck Fair takes place every year from 10 to 12 August in Killorglin, County Kerry, on the banks of the River Laune. Its organisers call it Ireland's oldest festival, with roots in the Gaelic calendar. In Irish it is Aonach an Phoic: the fair of the he-goat.

At its centre is a ritual found nowhere else. A wild mountain goat, a poc, is brought down into town, crowned King by a local girl chosen as Queen Puck, and raised onto a high stand above the town for the three days.

Around him, the whole town becomes the event: horse and cattle fairs, traditional and contemporary music, street performers, markets, a travelling funfair, children's events, packed pubs and twelve hours of free entertainment a day.

It is less a story about a goat than about a town. An August gathering older than any record became a chartered fair, became a livestock fair, became a three-day celebration, and somewhere along the way a goat became King.

What happens on each of the three days is on The Fair page.

A Chronology, Labelled Honestly

There is no single verified origin story. The fair is documented from 1613, evidence suggests an August fair before that, and the most famous story is a legend. This chronology follows the research of local historian Tom Doyle, published by the fair's organisers, and every entry carries one of four labels.

Documented

Supported by records or reliable sources.

Tradition

Passed down through generations.

Legend

Associated with Puck, not established as fact.

Unknown

The evidence doesn't allow a confident answer.

  1. Before the Records

    An August gathering with roots older than Killorglin itself.

  2. Pre-ChristianUnknown

    Lughnasa

    The first of August opened autumn in the old Irish calendar: Lughnasa, named for the god Lugh, one of the four turning points of the year with Imbolg, Bealtaine and Samhain. A harvest gathering at this time may be the fair's earliest layer, and the he-goat may have stood for fertility and plenty. It is a strong theory, but no record ties it to Killorglin's fair.

  3. 5th–7th centuriesDocumented

    Lammas Day

    As Christianity spread it grafted its own feasts onto the old ones. Lughnasa became Lammas, loaf-mass, when bread was blessed in thanks for the harvest. The 1613 patent would later name the fair for this day.

  4. To the 1840sDocumented

    The Last Lughnasa Gatherings

    Drung Hill, above the Glenbeigh to Cahersiveen road, was still a Lughnasa gathering place until the middle of the nineteenth century, when the Famine of 1845 to 1849 swept it away with so many other rural customs.

  5. Early Killorglin

    A church, a castle and a cluster of houses on the Laune.

  6. 258–1302Documented

    Cill Orglain

    The town's name honours Saint Lawrence, martyred on 10 August 258. The Farrantoreen Stone, an eighth-century cross slab near the bridge, is its earliest Christian relic. The Fitzgeralds built a castle on the Laune in 1215, a settlement grew on the Dromavalla side of the river by the 1280s, and its church appears in a 1302 inventory.

  7. 1587–1601Documented

    The Conways Arrive

    Jenkin Conway received Killorglin castle under the Plantation of Munster in 1587. It was destroyed in August 1600 and from 1601 he lived at Reen Lodge. The present town probably dates from this Elizabethan settlement.

  8. 1613Documented

    The Foundation Document

    Jenkin Conway's son, also Jenkin, obtained a patent to hold a fair “on Lammas Day and two days after”. It is the earliest solid documentary foundation for Puck Fair, and the cattle fair on Fair Day still runs under it. An earlier reference granting the Conways a sum for every animal brought to the August fair suggests the fair was already well established.

    The organisers' history page describes the 1613 document as a charter from King James I; Tom Doyle's chronology calls it a patent obtained from the Irish Parliament. We give both.

  9. 1650sDocumented

    The Blennerhassetts

    Robert Blennerhassett married Alice Conway and inherited the estate. The family would hold Killorglin, and the fair, for the next century and a half.

  10. 1659Documented

    A Village of Perhaps Eighty People

    The 1659 census recorded 26 people in Killorglin village and 123 in the countryside around it. Historians reckon the true figure was about three times that: around eighty people, a dozen households, forty-five years after the first Lammas fair. Tralee had over eight hundred.

  11. The Legend

    The story everyone tells, and why it can't be the beginning.

  12. 1640s–50sLegend

    The Goat Who Warned the Town

    Cromwell's soldiers scatter a herd of goats below the Reeks; one he-goat runs for Killorglin and raises the alarm. It is the best-known origin story, and the fair is documented decades before Cromwell's campaign, so it cannot explain how the fair began. Read the legend.

  13. The Eighteenth Century

    Landlords, calendars and a fair that nearly disappeared.

  14. 1690sDocumented

    Castleconway

    Captain John “Black Jack” Blennerhassett, released from prison after the Boyne, rebuilt a mansion on the castle site and renamed the village Castleconway. The name stuck for a century.

  15. 1752Documented

    The Fair Moves to 11 August

    When Britain and Ireland adopted the Gregorian calendar, eleven days vanished and Lammas Day moved with them. The three-day fair shifted from the first of August to the eleventh, where its middle day still sits.

  16. 1772Documented

    Three Livestock Fairs Instead

    Conway Blennerhassett, the last resident landlord, replaced the August fair with livestock fairs on 1 May, 1 June and 1 November, tied to his rent days. Whatever survived of the August gathering did so without its landlord.

  17. 1788Documented

    The First Newspaper Mention

    A letter from a Tralee resident about Rightboy agitation in Milltown and Killorglin, printed in the Freeman's Journal and the Dublin Evening Mail, is the first newspaper reference to Killorglin's August fair. It does not call it Puck Fair, which suggests the name had not yet attached itself.

  18. 1795Documented

    The Estate Is Sold

    Harman Blennerhassett sold the Manor of Killorglin to Thomas Mullins, later Baron Ventry, for £28,000. The new owners took little interest in the town and soon leased the fair's rights to the Foley family of Anglont.

  19. The Nineteenth Century

    A goat becomes King, and Puck becomes a national event.

  20. After 1795Unknown

    When Did the Crowning Begin?

    Research into the fair's chronology suggests the crowning of a he-goat probably began some time after the Blennerhassetts left in 1795 and 1796, rather than running unbroken from the 1600s. The goat carried satanic associations for the churches of the landlord class; a ribald symbol of seventy-two hours of merrymaking suited the town better than it would have suited them.

  21. Early 1800sTradition

    The Barons of the Fair and Their Mascot

    “Big Mick” Foley of Anglont, who held the fair's rights, was the “Baron of the Fair” in the popular imagination, the first of several generations to carry the title. The Foleys, enthusiastic faction fighters, kept a puck goat on a leash as a mascot to intimidate opponents. One view, set out in Seán Moraghan's 2013 history of the fair, is that the family turned the mascot into the fair's advertisement.

  22. 1808Tradition

    Tolls and a Loophole

    An Act of Parliament let the Viceroy ban tolls at cattle, horse and sheep fairs, and Harman Blennerhassett, out of favour in Dublin Castle, lost his. His lawyer, a young Daniel O'Connell, is said to have noticed that goats were not mentioned. The fair was advertised as a goat fair, and on 10 August 1808 a goat was hoisted onto a stage to prove it. Blennerhassett collected his tolls and Killorglin gained a King. We are looking for primary records.

  23. 1819Tradition

    “No Goat Was Crowned”

    A Killorglin man writing to the Kerry Sentinel in the 1890s as “The Octogenarian” recalled the town of his youth and noted that no goat was crowned in 1819 because of faction fighting. A recollection written seventy years on, but the earliest claim of a crowning we have.

  24. 1837Documented

    Faction Fighting at Puck

    A police report on a clash at the fair survives, reproduced in Kieran Foley's history of Killorglin.

  25. 1840sDocumented

    A Goat Is Crowned

    Regular reports of a goat being crowned King at Puck Fair appear from the 1840s onward. From here the tradition is continuous.

  26. 1880Documented

    The Land League Comes to Puck

    The Harringtons chose Killorglin on 11 August 1880, Fair Day, for the monster meeting that launched the Land League in Kerry. Nowhere else in the county would have given them a bigger crowd.

  27. 1885Documented

    The Railway

    The Great Southern and Western Railway opened its branch from Farranfore in January 1885. Dealers could now send cattle bought at Puck across Munster, Leinster and Connacht, and day-return tickets brought people from all over Kerry. The line closed in January 1960.

  28. 1800sDocumented

    Pubs, Tents and 72 Hours

    In the 1820s the town had three public houses and two streets. Breweries pitched tents for the fair, and the hotels and pubs put up cattle dealers the nights before and after. That is the origin of Puck's round-the-clock opening, trimmed to a three o'clock close only in 1968.

  29. Today

    The same three days, with the goat's welfare at the centre.

  30. 2013Documented

    Four Hundred Years

    Killorglin marks 400 years since the 1613 patent.

  31. Every AugustDocumented

    The Tradition Continues

    A goat handler catches a wild goat in the mountains, a schoolgirl chosen as Queen crowns him on 10 August, and on the 12th he is dethroned and taken home. The organisers say the goat's welfare comes first, with strict protocols checked by an independent veterinary surgeon.

Three layers, not one story. An ancient Celtic layer (Lughnasa, the harvest, the symbolic goat). A historical layer (a documented August fair, chartered in 1613). And the King Puck legend, with the nineteenth-century tradition of crowning a real goat. The story told today isn't necessarily the origin of Puck Fair. It's a mythology that grew around a much older fair.

The Honest Answer: Nobody Knows for Sure

Puck is the male goat, and Puck Fair is simply the fair of the he-goat. Why he became King has three answers.

The oldest: at a harvest-time gathering, a male goat stood for fertility and plenty, like the god Pan. Theory

The most famous: he warned the town of Cromwell's army, and the crown is the town's thanks. Legend

The most practical: in 1808 the fair became a goat fair to escape a toll on other livestock, and a goat was raised on a platform to prove it. Tradition

The historian's answer adds a fourth: in early nineteenth-century Ireland the goat was a ribald figure, and in Killorglin a way of hinting at the bacchanalian side of a three-day fair. The Foleys' mascot goat may simply have been good advertising. Tradition

What is certain is that the goat is wild, from the mountains around Killorglin, and that the town has crowned one every August since at least the 1840s.

The Mountain, the Goat, the Warning Legend

The story is told like this. During Oliver Cromwell's campaign in Ireland, his soldiers were pillaging the countryside around Shanara and Kilgobnet at the foot of the McGillycuddy's Reeks when they came upon a herd of wild goats grazing on the upland and scattered them.

One male goat, the Puck, broke away. Instead of running up into the mountains with the others, he ran towards Killorglin on the banks of the Laune, exhausted and frightened. When the townspeople saw him arrive they knew danger was coming. They protected their livestock, prepared themselves, and escaped the worst of it.

They never forgot what the goat had done. They began to honour him every year, and in time he became King Puck.

It's a fantastic story, but there is no contemporary evidence for it, and the fair is documented before Cromwell's campaign, so it can't explain how the fair began. It is the most-told story about Puck, and stories are part of the archive too. More legends and lore.
